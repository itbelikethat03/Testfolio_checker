Two studies, one comparison surface

90/60 and the Kelly Ladder

Both studies ask what leverage buys you. One levers bonds inside an equity portfolio and asks whether the term premium pays for it. The other levers equities and asks how much of the growth-optimal answer rests on a handful of days. This page puts their portfolios on one set of axes — and refuses the comparisons the data cannot support.

05 · What actually moves the answer

Sensitivity

The 90/60 edge reduces to one identity: 0.60 × bond term premium − 0.10 × equity risk premium − costs. Because of that 6:1 multiplier, the bond row of the grid decides everything and the equity column barely matters.

07 · Fractional Kelly vs best days

Is Quarter Kelly a stand-in for losing the best days?

Two different ways to be more conservative about the same history. Fractional Kelly scales exposure and keeps every day. Best-day removal keeps full exposure and deletes the 10, 25 or 100 strongest days (ranked on unlevered return, the rest kept in date order). This section tests, across many metrics at once, whether Half or Quarter Kelly ends up looking like Full Kelly with its best days gone. The hypothesis is tested, not assumed. 09 · Reading these together

What the combination does and does not license

These portfolios are not interchangeable

The Efficient Core series are developed-market, USD, 1990–2026. The Kelly series are US-only research portfolios, 1926–2026, gross of every cost. Scoring them with the same function makes the metrics comparable; it does not make the markets comparable.

The one case where this bites hardest: US small-cap value carries no cost drag at all. The Kelly study's own dfsvx_compare.py puts the implied drag at ≈1.09%/yr, and its +0.128 daily autocorrelation inflates measured geometric growth further. Every US small-value figure on this page is optimistic by at least that much.

Best-day removal is a counterfactual, not a forecast

Selecting “the best days” uses full-sample look-ahead by construction. It measures how sensitive a mean-driven estimator is to positive-tail observations — nothing more.

The scenario is also internally incoherent: 95% of the market's 20 best days occurred inside a drawdown deeper than 20%, and 45% fell within ten trading days of one of the 20 worst days. A world without the best days but with the worst days intact could not have existed.

Where the two studies agree

Both land on the same structural point from opposite directions: leverage applied to a low-volatility, low-correlation leg is cheap in risk terms; leverage applied to the volatile leg is expensive. The Efficient Core study measures it as variance drag — 1.5× equity loses 1.89pp/yr to it against 90/60's 0.45pp. The Kelly study measures it as a drawdown budget — small-cap value earns a higher growth-optimal leverage (2.78× vs 2.62×) but a lower risk-budgeted one (0.70× vs 0.90×), because its volatility breaches the budget sooner.

And where they point in opposite directions

The Efficient Core study's best risk-adjusted result over 36 years did not come from leverage at all — developed small-cap value returned a higher CAGR (10.90%) and a higher Sharpe (0.69) than the 90/60 structure. The Kelly study then shows that the same factor, in the US, carries the lower drawdown-budgeted leverage of the two series it tests. Read together: the factor is the better unlevered holding and the worse thing to lever.
